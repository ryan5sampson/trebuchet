Plate I · What is it?

A machine that throws castles at castles

A trebuchet (say it TREB-yoo-shay) is a giant medieval siege engine. It uses a long, swinging arm and a sling to hurl heavy stones at, and over, enemy walls.

The big ones run on gravity. Crews winch a huge box of rocks or earth (the counterweight) high into the air. When they pull the trigger, the box falls, the arm whips around, and the sling flings the stone hundreds of meters.

For about 300 years, from the 1100s to the 1400s, the counterweight trebuchet was the most powerful weapon on Earth.

Plate II · History

Two thousand years of throwing rocks

The trebuchet started in ancient China as a people-powered machine, traveled west along trade and war routes, and peaked in medieval Europe and the Middle East.

c. 400 BC · China

Traction trebuchets

Chinese texts describe a beam on a pivot with ropes on one end. A crew of dozens of people yanks the ropes all at once to fling the stone.

Late 500s AD · Byzantium

The idea moves west

Traction trebuchets reach the Mediterranean, likely via the Avars. Byzantine writers record them at the siege of Thessalonica.

1100s · Mediterranean

The counterweight arrives

Byzantine and Arab engineers swap the rope crew for a heavy falling weight. Now one machine can throw stones far heavier than people could ever pull.

1189 – 1191 · Acre

The Third Crusade

At the siege of Acre, crusader kings batter the walls with named trebuchets, including "Bad Neighbour" and "God's Own Sling."

1268 – 1273 · Xiangyang, China

Back where it began

Kublai Khan brings in two Persian engineers to build counterweight trebuchets. A city that held out for five years surrenders soon after.

1304 · Stirling Castle, Scotland

The Warwolf

England's King Edward I builds the biggest trebuchet of its day. The Scots try to surrender before it's finished. Edward refuses until he has tested it on the walls.

1300s – 1400s · Europe

Gunpowder takes over

Cannons first appear in a 1326 English manuscript. By 1453, giant Ottoman cannons smash the walls of Constantinople.

1521 · Tenochtitlan, Mexico

The last shot

Low on gunpowder, Hernán Cortés's men build a trebuchet. Its first stone flies straight up and crashes back down onto the machine.

Plate III · Trebuchet vs. catapult

Every trebuchet is a catapult. Not every catapult is a trebuchet.

Catapult is the umbrella word for any machine that throws things without gunpowder. What sets each type apart is where it stores its energy.

Ballista

Tension: bent arms pull a cord back like a giant crossbow. Fires bolts flat and accurately.

Mangonel / onager

Torsion: a bundle of twisted rope or sinew snaps the arm forward. Small and quick, but limited by how much rope can twist.

Trebuchet

Gravity (or people pulling): a falling weight swings a lever and sling. Bigger weight, bigger throw. No rope to wear out.

Plate IV · Types

A family of throwing machines

c. 400 BC – 1200s

Traction

The original. Teams of 15 to 250 people pull ropes on the short end. Fast to reload: several shots a minute. But people can only pull so hard.

1100s

Hybrid

A small counterweight helps the pulling crew. This is the in-between step that led to pure gravity power.

1100s – 1400s

Fixed counterweight

The weight is bolted solid to the arm. Simple and strong, but the weight swings in an arc, which wastes some energy.

1200s – 1400s

Hinged counterweight

The box hangs from a hinge so it can drop more nearly straight down. More of its energy goes into the throw. The design of most great medieval trebuchets.

1300s – 1400s

Couillard

Two smaller counterweights hang on either side of a single upright. Lighter and faster to build and move.

Modern

Floating arm

A hobby and competition design. The axle rolls along a track while the weight drops straight down, so almost no energy is wasted.

Plate VI · How it works

Turning a falling box into a flying rock

A trebuchet is an energy converter. It stores energy slowly (crews winch the weight up over minutes) and releases it in about one second.

Store

PE = m · g · h

Lifting the counterweight stores gravitational potential energy. Double the weight or the height and you double the energy.

Lever

long arm ÷ short arm ≈ 4–6

The axle is a fulcrum. The long end travels about five times farther than the short end in the same time, so it moves about five times faster.

Whip

arm + sling ≈ a longer arm

The sling swings out like the tip of a whip. It acts like a longer arm and can roughly double the throw compared with no sling.

Release

range ≈ v² · sin(2θ) ÷ g

The sling slips off the release pin. Without air, a launch angle (θ) of 45° goes farthest. Real crews aimed by adjusting the pin.

Try the numbers

A 10,000 kg counterweight falls 5 m:
10,000 × 9.8 × 5 ≈ 490,000 J

If about half of that reaches a 100 kg stone, the stone leaves at about 70 m/s (250 km/h).

On paper, that flies about 500 m. Air drag, friction, and an imperfect release cut it to the 150–300 m that real machines managed.

Plate VIII · Impact

How one machine changed the world

Architecture

Castles got tougher

Builders answered with thicker walls, round towers that deflect stones, and rings of walls inside walls (concentric castles). Many castles mounted their own trebuchets on towers to fire back.

Warfare

Sieges got shorter

Before, armies often just waited for a city to starve. Counterweight trebuchets could knock down walls, so cities like Acre and Xiangyang fell faster.

Terror

More than rocks

Armies threw fire pots, rotting animals, and the heads of captives. One account says that at Kaffa in 1346 attackers flung plague victims over the walls.

Language

The first "engineers"

Medieval Latin called a siege machine an ingenium, and the person who built it an ingeniator. That's where the English word engineer comes from.

Science

Thinking in motion

Designing trebuchets pushed people to reason about levers, weights, and flight paths, ideas later made precise by physicists like Galileo and Newton.

Today

A favorite STEM project

Schools, castles, and pumpkin-throwing contests build trebuchets to teach energy, levers, and projectile motion.

Plate IX · Build one

A tabletop trebuchet

About an hour, cheap materials, and a real lesson in energy. Works with one per group.

Materials

  • About 20 craft sticks or unsharpened pencils
  • A wooden skewer or dowel (the axle)
  • Hot glue or rubber bands
  • A paper cup or small bag + coins or washers (counterweight)
  • String and a 5 cm square of cloth or paper (sling)
  • A paper clip (release pin)
  • Mini marshmallows or foil balls (ammo)

Steps

  1. Glue a rectangle of sticks flat for the base.
  2. Build two triangles (A-frames) and stand them on the base, a bit wider than one stick apart.
  3. Poke the skewer through both peaks so it spins freely.
  4. Attach the arm to the skewer so the long side is 4 to 5 times longer than the short side.
  5. Hang the cup from the short end with a loop of string so it swings.
  6. Bend the paper clip into a hook and tape it to the tip of the long end, pointing forward.
  7. Make the sling: tie one string to the arm tip and the other to a small loop that slips over the hook. Put the cloth pouch in the middle. Make it about as long as the long arm.
  8. Load the ammo, pull the arm down, aim, and let go.

Tuning tips

  • Goes straight up? Bend the hook more closed so it lets go later.
  • Slams into the floor? Open the hook so it lets go sooner.
  • Not enough power? Add weight, or lengthen the long arm.
  • Experiment: change one thing at a time and measure the distance.

Safety

  • Soft ammo only. Wear eye protection.
  • Never stand in front of, or lean over, a loaded trebuchet.
  • Keep fingers clear of the arm's swing path.
Plate X · Why we stopped

Gunpowder changed everything

Cannons arrived in the 1300s. Within about 150 years they had pushed trebuchets off the battlefield.

  • Size: a big trebuchet needed tonnes of timber and had to be built on site, often over weeks. A cannon arrived ready to fire.
  • Speed: winching a counterweight takes minutes. Early cannons were slow too, but improved fast.
  • Hitting power: a trebuchet lobs stones in a high arc. A cannon fires low and fast, so it can pound the base of a wall until it collapses.
  • Defense: cannons led to low, thick, star-shaped forts that trebuchet stones barely scratched.

The family tree of indirect fire

The trebuchet's real job, throwing something up and over a wall, never went away. Each new weapon did it farther and faster.

1100s

Trebuchet

Gravity. ~300 m.

1400s

Bombard

Huge early cannon that smashes walls.

1500s →

Mortar

Short cannon that lobs shells high over walls. The trebuchet's closest heir.

1800s →

Howitzer

Long-range artillery, many kilometers.

1900s →

Rockets & missiles

Carry their own fuel. Hundreds of kilometers or more.
